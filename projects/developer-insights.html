Developer Insights Analysis

What a global developer survey says about pay, satisfaction and language adoption

Overview

An analysis of a large public global developer survey, taken from the raw export through to conclusions about pay, job satisfaction and language adoption. The interesting part of this project is not the charts at the end — it is everything that had to happen before the charts were allowed to mean anything.

The problem

Survey exports are messy in specific, predictable ways. Compensation arrives as free text in a dozen currencies. Multi-select questions are packed into single delimited columns, so one cell holds five answers. Country and job-title fields are inconsistent enough that a naive groupby silently splits the same group in two.

Every one of those problems produces a plausible-looking chart. That is what makes them dangerous: nothing errors out, the numbers are just quietly wrong. Most of the work here was making the data trustworthy enough that a conclusion drawn from it could be defended.

The data

A public developer survey export: tens of thousands of responses across hundreds of columns, with mixed types throughout and heavy missingness concentrated in exactly the fields that matter most — compensation and years of experience. Missingness there is not random, which rules out dropping incomplete rows and moving on.

Approach

  • Normalised and type-cast columns first, so that every later step operated on known types rather than on whatever the CSV reader guessed.
  • Split the multi-select fields into proper boolean columns, which is what makes language adoption countable at all.
  • Handled salary outliers explicitly rather than silently dropping them — an outlier in compensation data is often a real, interesting response rather than an error, and deleting it quietly changes the answer.
  • Ran correlation analysis across satisfaction, compensation and experience.
  • Visualised distributions with Matplotlib and Seaborn instead of reporting means. A mean hides a bimodal distribution completely, and compensation data is rarely a single clean hump.

Results

The cleaned dataset surfaced readable trends in job satisfaction, in how compensation is distributed rather than just centred, and in which languages are gaining or losing adoption across the respondent base. The distribution plots carried more information than any summary statistic did: several fields that looked unremarkable as an average turned out to be clearly split into distinct groups.

Exact figures live in the notebook in the repository rather than being restated here, because they depend on the specific survey year and the filtering choices documented alongside them.

What I would do differently

  • The cleaning logic lives in notebook cells. It belongs in a tested module so the whole analysis can be reproduced from the raw file with one command — as it stands, reproducing it means running cells in the right order and trusting that I did.
  • I chose the outlier thresholds by inspecting the distributions. That worked, but it is a judgement call I cannot fully defend after the fact. A stated rule, decided before looking at the data, would be more honest.
  • I reported correlations without significance testing. With this sample size most of them hold up, but reporting a coefficient without a confidence interval invites more certainty than the analysis earned.

Tech stack

Python, pandas and NumPy for the cleaning and analysis; Matplotlib and Seaborn for the visualisation; scikit-learn for the statistical work; Jupyter as the working environment.
