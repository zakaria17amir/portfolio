Sales & Menu Analysis with PySpark

Scalable ETL and data quality checks on Databricks

  • PySpark
  • Apache Spark
  • Databricks
  • SQL
  • ETL

View code on GitHub Back to portfolio

Overview

Distributed ETL over sales and menu data in Databricks, ending in an analytical view of customer spend and revenue trends. The goal was to build the pipeline the way it would have to be built if someone were going to report from it — with checks between the stages, not just at the end.

The problem

The analysis needs sales joined to menu and customer data, at a volume where single-machine pandas stops being the right tool. Moving to Spark solves the volume problem and introduces a different one: joins that fail partially are much harder to notice. A join that silently drops a tenth of the rows still produces a complete-looking result set.

The data

Sales transactions joined to a menu and product table and to customer records. The join keys are the fragile part — they are where inconsistent identifiers, duplicates and missing references all surface, and where a quiet failure does the most damage to the final numbers.

Approach

  • Ingested into Spark DataFrames with explicit schemas rather than inferred ones. Schema inference is convenient and it will change types on you between runs when the sample happens to differ.
  • Put quality checks between stages: row counts before and after each join, null checks on the join keys, and duplicate detection on what should be unique.
  • Aggregated spend and revenue by customer and by product once the inputs were known to be sound.
  • Surfaced the aggregates in an interactive dashboard so the output is explorable rather than a static table.

Results

The pipeline runs end to end on Databricks and produces the aggregated spend and revenue views. More usefully, the row-count checks around the joins mean a partial join shows up as a failed check rather than as a number that is quietly ten per cent too low.

What I would do differently

  • The quality checks are assertions inside the notebook rather than a pipeline stage that can fail a run. Something like Great Expectations would make a failure explicit and recorded instead of depending on someone reading the cell output.
  • The job is not idempotent. Re-running it over the same input does not cleanly replace the previous output, which is fine for an analysis and would not be acceptable on a schedule.
  • I left partitioning at the defaults. That is genuinely fine at this data size, and it is the first thing that would need attention at ten times the volume — I would rather say that plainly than pretend I had tuned it.

Tech stack

PySpark and Apache Spark for the distributed processing, Databricks as the platform, and SQL for the aggregation logic.
